Things I can't unnotice

There's a specific kind of curse where you start seeing something, and then you can't stop. Not a hallucination. Just — a pattern. A tension. A tiny thing that keeps happening and nobody else seems to find weird.

It started in the lab. I'd be writing a deviation report — formal, templated, everything in its right place — and I'd notice that the way the form was structured basically decided the answer before I'd finished thinking. The boxes wanted a cause. One cause. Something identifiable, documentable, closeable. And I'd be sitting there going: but it was three things. It was always three things.

I mentioned this to a colleague once. She looked at me like I'd said something mildly unhinged. "That's just how the form works," she said, and went back to her coffee. Which, fair. The form does work. Deviations get closed. The system keeps running.

I've since learned this is called premature accountability convergence. I've written a paper about it. I'm still not over it.

But that's the thing about noticing too hard — once you see it, it's everywhere. The meeting where someone says "we need to find who's responsible" before anyone's figured out what actually happened. The SOP that's seventeen pages long and written by someone who clearly did not have to use it. The investigation that takes three days and lands on one person's name, neat and tidy, filed and forgotten.

I'm not saying the systems are evil. I'm saying they're designed, and design has consequences, and most of the time nobody's asking what those consequences are.

So I started asking. Quietly at first — just me and a notebook and too many iced americanos. Then less quietly. Then with data. Then with five prototypes and nine papers and a 4S Toronto abstract that I submitted last week while aggressively not refreshing my email.

(I refreshed it fourteen times. Decent latte art though. Rosetta. I'm working on tulips.)

The curse of noticing is that you can't really unknow a thing. Once you've seen how a form can close down thinking, you see it in every form. Once you know that loopbacks in document navigation aren't user error but structural friction — you feel it every time you're looking for an SOP at 4pm and you can't find it and you just ask a colleague instead because it's faster.

That gap — between what the system says should happen and what actually happens — is where I live now. It's not a comfortable place. But it's a genuinely interesting one.

And honestly? I can't think of anywhere I'd rather be. ☕
