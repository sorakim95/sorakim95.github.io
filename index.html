AI-mediated representations in regulated investigations reorganize when judgment closes and how responsibility stabilizes. Sora Kim
I notice things a little too hard — patterns, tensions, the quiet friction no one names. Then I write them down, and keep turning them over until they become something worth keeping.

9-to-6, I'm a QC Project Lead. 6-to-9, I'm a researcher. It's a strange way to live, but an interesting one.
Seoul 🌱 HCI · STS always noticing
how I got here
I started thinking seriously about accountability because I was living inside it every day — navigating GMP systems, writing deviation reports, watching how formal structures shaped what could and couldn't be said about what went wrong.

Aviation has human factors research. Healthcare has patient safety UX. Finance has interface compliance design. Pharmaceutical QC workers operate inside some of the most tightly regulated environments on earth — and somehow, the question of how that system design shapes their reasoning, judgment, and accountability has stayed mostly unasked.

That gap is where this research program lives.
research focus
I study how accountability is structured in regulated work — and how system design shapes which causal explanations remain available before responsibility settles. My work combines HCI experimentation with STS analysis, using pharmaceutical GMP environments as a primary empirical site.

Reasoning · R1 · Pilot Study
Expanding Causal Reasoning under Closure Pressure
AI scaffoldreasoningCSCW 2026preprint
Description
Premature causal convergence in GMP investigations can be shaped by closure pressure — reasoning may narrow before alternative contributors are fully explored. This study deploys a structured AI scaffold as a comparison enforcer, keeping alternative explanations visible long enough to delay premature convergence without automating the conclusion.
Key Findings
18/20 participants broadened causal reasoning when scaffold was active
15/20 reconsidered initial attribution after scaffolded comparison
Scaffold use delayed closure and expanded the range of comparable explanations

Cognitive Friction in Procedural Documentation
corpuslanguageSTS+HCIpreprint
Description
Procedural sentences embed structurally produced judgment demands through conditional branching, cross-references, exception structures, and discretionary qualifiers. A rule-based corpus analysis operationalizes cognitive friction as a measurable sentence-level property of procedural language.
Key Findings
Conditional branching was the most prevalent friction feature (33.9%)
18.6% of sentences exhibited co-occurring features — structurally compounded judgment demand
