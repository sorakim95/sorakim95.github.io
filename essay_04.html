On learning languages badly

I'm learning French from a cartoon rat and Spanish from a Puerto Rican reggaeton artist. My Duolingo streak is zero. I regret nothing.

It started with Ratatouille. I watched it again — for maybe the eighth time, I've lost count — and there was something about hearing «tout le monde peut cuisiner» that hit differently than "anyone can cook." Something about the rhythm of it. The way French makes even a cartoon rat sound like he's saying something genuinely important.

So I looked it up. And then I looked up another phrase. And then I was forty minutes deep into a rabbit hole about French pronunciation and I'd completely forgotten I was supposed to be revising a paper.

This is how I learn things. Accidentally, sideways, starting from something completely unrelated. I've made peace with it.

The Spanish happened separately. Bad Bunny — reggaeton, not rap, an important distinction — came on shuffle and I realized I understood maybe thirty percent of «Tití Me Preguntó» and that felt like completely unacceptable. So I started looking up lyrics. Then I started listening more carefully. Then I was lip-syncing in the kitchen at 11pm, which is a sentence I did not expect to be writing in 2026 but here we are.

I did get one compliment on my French, to be fair. I was living in Boston for a while and had a French friend who was very patient about my pronunciation — which was, by all accounts, not good. But apparently my croissant, baguette and ratatouille were perfect. He was very excited about this. I think it says everything you need to know about my priorities that the three words I pronounce correctly are two baked goods and an animated film. I really like bread. And Ratatouille.

My Japanese is fluent enough to argue — I lived in Tokyo for a year and a half, picked most of it up working part-time jobs. Nothing accelerates vocabulary acquisition like a customer who wants something and you have approximately three seconds to figure out what. Significantly more effective than any textbook, and also more terrifying.

The thing is, I don't think I'm actually learning these languages in any useful, measurable way. I can say «tu me manques» and explain that it literally means "you are missing from me" which is a much more interesting way to talk about missing someone. I can tell you that Bad Bunny's flow does something rhythmically that I can't fully articulate but can feel. Neither of these things will help me in a French café or a Spanish conversation.

But I think what I'm actually doing — what I'm always doing, really — is noticing. How a language moves. What it prioritizes. What it makes easy to say versus what it makes you work for. French makes you slow down. Spanish makes you commit. Korean, my first language, still surprises me sometimes — I'll find a word that does something English doesn't have a shape for and I'll sit with it for a while, just pleased that it exists.

«Tout le monde peut cuisiner.» Anyone can cook. Anyone can notice. Anyone can find a way in through a cartoon or a song or a word that lands exactly right.

Gusteau would approve, I think. 🐀
